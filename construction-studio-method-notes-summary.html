Method notes in brief

The short version: what the tool computes, and what it does not model at all. It sizes members from span rules of thumb and takes load down by tributary area. There is no solver in it — no stiffness matrix, no finite elements, nothing iterative, and no internal force is computed anywhere. The full method notes give all 27 rules, the measured tables and the provenance of every figure.

Audience Architecture students and staff Method Span rules of thumb · tributary-area takedown Runs Entirely in the browser Version 1.0.1 · 14 September 2026

Three things to know before you cite a number

  1. The panel prints a range; the tool sizes from the middle of it. A depth rule is the span divided by a number. A steel beam is labelled L/18 – L/20, which on a 12 m span means 667 mm deep at one end and 600 mm at the other — a real choice, since the shallow end costs steel and the deep end costs headroom. The tool makes it for you: it averages the two divisors and uses 19, so the panel reads 12.00 m ÷ 19 = 632 mm → IPE 600, a depth the range it quotes never names. Seven rules work this way. Quoting a depth from this tool as “L/18” is wrong: it is the midpoint, always.
  2. Nothing here is analysis. No strength, deflection, buckling, fire or connection check is ever performed. The green / amber / red flag compares the span against the range the rule suits — not the member against a capacity. A green flag does not mean the member works.
  3. The load takedown is gravity only, by tributary area. No frame action, no continuity, no moment, no wind, no seismic, no snow. It is the hand calculation done at the start of a project, and nothing more.

What it calculates

Five things, and they are all arithmetic you could do on paper.

Columns, posts and piers all share one shape — gather a load, divide by a permissible stress, take the square root for a square section:

N = A_trib × max(1, storeys) × w kN A_required = N / f_permissible steel f = 0.6 × 275 MPa (S275) RC f = 0.4 × 30 MPa (C30) timber f = 0.6 × 24 MPa (GL24) masonry f = 2.5 MPa (pier)

The load w is one number per use class, applied as a single combined allowance per square metre per storey: residential 9, office 11, retail 12, industrial 14, roof 5 kN/m². Dead and imposed are never separated and no load factors are applied, so these are service-level allowances, not design values. They cannot be carried into a code calculation as they stand.

Load comes down top-down. Where the vertical system offers lines — walls — it is spread evenly along the perimeter, so every metre of wall carries the same whatever is above it. Where it offers points — columns — each grid node takes the floor area nearest to it. Two invariants are asserted by the test suite: the loads on all supports add up to the loads on all floors, and the reactions every level’s members deliver add up to that level’s load.

Members are classified by their own direction rather than by name, so a rafter, a brace and a portal leg classify themselves. A member takes the strip of floor its family spacing implies and delivers half of what it collects to each end — the classic wL/2. An inclined member also pushes its support sideways, and that push is the whole reason a pitched roof needs a tie:

H = V × (run / rise) = V / tan(pitch)

The strips are normalised so they add back up to the level’s load, which means an error in any one member’s assumed strip is redistributed among the others rather than showing up as a shortfall. The arithmetic always balances; that it balances is not evidence that any individual member’s share is right.

The flag is green far more often than it should be

Eight of the 27 rules can never return anything but green, because they carry neither a span band to compare against nor a real check: the four column rules, three of the four foundation rules, and the pad footing — which does have a check, but one written to compare a constant zero against a limit of one, so it can only ever pass. Only the masonry slenderness rule performs a genuine check. The other eighteen compare the span against the range the rule suits, which is useful, but it is a statement about applicability, not about the member.

The consequence is worth seeing concretely. HEB is the European wide-flange H-section series the tool draws its steel columns from: near-square in plan, so it resists buckling about either axis equally, and numbered by its depth in millimetres. An HEB 600 is 600 mm deep with 300 mm flanges and 27 000 mm² of steel in its cross-section. Here is an office column on a 7.5 × 7.5 m grid, sized by the tool:

Conservative one way, unconservative the other

runs high
No imposed-load reduction
Every storey is counted at its full allowance. Real practice reduces the imposed component on a column gathering many floors, because the chance of every floor being fully loaded at once is small. The tool's loads therefore run high — increasingly so with height.
runs low
No slenderness reduction
Column area uses a flat permissible stress with no reduction for buckling. A slender column is therefore undersized by this rule — in the opposite direction to the point above. The two do not cancel in any predictable way.
neither
One combined load
Dead and imposed are never separated and no partial factors are applied, so the figures are neither characteristic nor design values.
neither
Rounding
Concrete to 25 mm, glulam to 45 mm — one lamination — masonry to 5 mm, and catalogue families to the next section up. Panels are synthesised at the exact size wanted, so a CLT or hollowcore depth can look more precise than the rule behind it.

One more worth knowing before you quote a masonry figure. The slenderness limit of 27 is the Eurocode 6 value, but the cavity-wall effective thickness the tool uses, t_ef = ⅔(t₁ + t₂), is the BS 5628 form; EN 1996-1-1 gives (t₁³ + t₂³)1/3. The source names only “EN 1996”, with no clause and no edition, so which was intended cannot be read from the code. As a rule of thumb this changes little; quoted as a code check it matters.

What can be checked, is: 27 sizing checks against hand calculation, 18 load-takedown checks in both directions including the eaves thrust, the generator exercised across all 30 systems and every cross-part combination, and 71 checks driving the app in a real browser. That tells you the tool computes what it says it computes. It says nothing about whether a rule of thumb suits your building.

Not modelled at all

These are the assumptions a student is most likely to make wrongly, because a general-purpose structural tool would handle them.

Out
Structural analysis
No solver of any kind: no stiffness matrix, no finite elements, no moment, no shear force, no bending diagram. Nothing in the application computes an internal force.
Out
Strength and deflection checks
No member is ever verified against a capacity or a deflection limit. Several rules note in their own source text that deflection normally governs — and then do not check it.
Out
Buckling, fire and connection design
Named in the tool's own disclaimer as absent, and absent they are.
Out
Lateral load
No wind, no seismic, no notional horizontal force. Braced frames, shear walls and portal frames are drawn and named, but nothing sizes their bracing or checks the building's stability.
Out
Snow and load combinations
One combined allowance per square metre per use class, at service level. No partial factors, no combination formed.
Out
Reinforcement
No bar is sized, counted or arranged. Concrete elements are solid volumes.
Out
Building physics
No U-value, no thermal bridge, no condensation risk, no acoustics, no airtightness. The insulation layer in a build-up has a thickness and a colour and nothing else — the tool holds no material conductivity data at all.
Out
Cost and carbon
No quantities are priced and no embodied carbon is calculated, though the element counts a take-off would need are present in the model.
Note
Geometry
One rectangular footprint on an orthogonal grid. No L-plans, no curves, no setbacks, no atria, no double-height spaces, no stairs or cores as modelled objects — and no drawing tool: everything you see is generated or edited, never drawn.

Not acceptable for any part of any regulatory submission — not Part L, not Part A, not anything else. For Part L the question does not arise, since no thermal quantity exists anywhere in the tool. For structure, the application’s own on-screen disclaimer is the correct statement of its status: “These are rules of thumb for learning and for early design. They are not analysis and not design. Nothing here checks strength, stability, deflection, fire, buckling or connections.”

Where it stops

This tool sits between a sketch and a scheme. It is more rigorous than a sketch, because the geometry is real and the arithmetic balances. It is far less rigorous than a scheme design, because nothing is checked. Use it to understand relationships — how depth follows span, how load accumulates, how a system choice propagates through a building — and use something else to find out whether the building stands up.

Against an analysis package it will diverge, and no setting closes the gap. There is no solver to configure. A real frame sheds load through continuity and moment connections; here every support takes only what falls nearest to it, so interior columns come out under-loaded and edge columns over-loaded relative to an analysed frame. Bracing and cores are drawn but carry nothing. Connections are named and shaped, not designed.

Cite it as a teaching model of construction assembly and load path. Do not cite it as a source of material property data — the code names no source for those values — and do not cite it for building physics at all, because there is none. For anything that must stand up, use an engineer and an analysis package.

Quote differences, not absolute values. These are rules of thumb rounded to catalogue sizes. A change that moves a section by one size is a result; a 5% change in a computed depth is the rule’s own imprecision, not a finding.
