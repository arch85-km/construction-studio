Construction Studio Method Notes

What this tool computes, how it computes it, and — the part that matters most when you cite it — what it does not model at all. Everything below is read from the source code, not from what a structural modelling tool is generally assumed to do. Where the code makes something unclear, this page says so rather than inferring.

Audience Architecture students and staff Method Span rules of thumb · tributary-area takedown Runs Entirely in the browser

Three things to know before you cite a number

  1. The panel prints a range; the tool computes one number from the middle of it. A steel beam is labelled L/18 – L/20 and then sized at L/19. Seven of the rules work this way. Quoting a depth from this tool as "L/18" is wrong — it is the midpoint, always.
  2. Nothing here is analysis. No strength, deflection, buckling, fire or connection check is ever performed. The green / amber / red flag compares the span against the range the rule suits, not the member against a capacity.
  3. The load takedown is gravity only, by tributary area. No frame action, no continuity, no moment, no wind, no seismic, no snow. It is the hand calculation done at the start of a project, and nothing more.

Member depth, and the midpoint problem

Each rule carries a ratio string for the panel and a depth function for the arithmetic. They are not the same thing. Where the ratio is a range, the function divides by its midpoint and the range is never computed:

RuleShown in the panelActually divided by
Steel beamL/18 – L/2019
Composite steel beamL/22 – L/2523.5
RC beamh = L/12 – L/1513.5
Precast hollowcoreL/35 – L/4037.5
Timber joistL/15 – L/2017.5
Timber trussL/5 – L/65.5
Portal frame columnh/15 – h/2520
 The spread matters: L/18 and L/20 differ by 11%, which on a 12 m span is 63 mm — often a whole section size. The tool never shows you the ends of the range it quotes.

Two rules go further: their ratio string does not describe the depth at all. Purlin is labelled "spacing ≤ 1.8 m" and then sized at L/40. Portal frame rafter is labelled with its haunch proportions and then sized at L/55. Neither divisor appears anywhere in the interface. A third, the one-way slab, prints "L/24 one end continuous · L/28 both ends" but carries an undocumented third case: with no continuity at all it divides by 20.

Columns, posts and piers

All four column rules share one shape — accumulate a load, divide by a permissible stress, take the square root for a square section:

N = A_trib × max(1, storeys) × w kN A_required = N / f_permissible steel f = 0.6 × 275 MPa (S275) RC f = 0.4 × 30 MPa (C30) timber f = 0.6 × 24 MPa (GL24) masonry f = 2.5 MPa (pier)

There is no imposed-load reduction. A real takedown reduces the imposed load on a column carrying many storeys, because the chance of every floor being fully loaded at once is small; EN 1991-1-1 gives the factor. This tool multiplies by the full storey count every time, so tall buildings are sized conservatively — increasingly so the taller they get.

Imposed loading

One number per use class, applied as a single combined load per square metre per storey. Dead and imposed are not separated, and no load factors are applied — these are service-level allowances, not design values.

The load takedown

Computed top down. Each level contributes its whole plan area times its load; the running total is what arrives at each storey. How it is shared depends on what the vertical system declares it offers:

Vertical system providesLoad is shared
lines (walls)Spread evenly along the footprint perimeter, 2(W + D). Every metre of wall carries the same load, whatever is above it.
points (columns)Each grid node takes the floor area nearest to it — its tributary area — accumulated down the building.

Two invariants hold and are asserted by the test suite: the loads on all supports must add up to the loads on all floors, and the reactions every level's members deliver must add up to that level's load. Nothing is lost going down, and nothing is lost going sideways.

Where load travels along the structure

Every member is classified by its own direction rather than by its name, so a rafter, a brace and a portal leg classify themselves and so will anything added later:

|u_y| > 0.94 vertical |u_y| < 0.09 horizontal otherwise inclined

A member takes the strip of floor its own family spacing implies, delivers half of what it collects to each end — the classic wL/2 — and an inclined member also pushes its support sideways. That horizontal push is the whole reason a pitched roof needs a tie:

H = V × (run / rise) = V / tan(pitch)

The strips are then normalised so they add back up to the level's load — which means an error in any one member's assumed strip is redistributed among the others rather than showing up as a shortfall. The arithmetic always balances; that it balances is not evidence that any individual member's share is right.

The masonry check

h_ef / t_ef ≤ 27 (≤ 20 where a leaf is under 90 mm and there are 2+ storeys) t_ef = ⅔(t₁ + t₂) cavity wall — both leaves count

Attributed in the source to EN 1996. It assumes hef is the clear storey height — a wall held top and bottom and free at the sides. Real restraint factors are not modelled. The tool says so on screen, which is better teaching than hiding it.

02

How the building is generated

You do not draw the building. You choose a system for each of five parts and set a grid; everything else is derived. Changing any choice rebuilds the whole model.

Roof6Metal deck · portal rafters · trussed rafters · cut rafters · concrete flat · CLT flat
 Thirty systems. The ground floor is a separate choice because it behaves nothing like an upper floor — a ground-bearing slab does not span at all.

Why some combinations are refused

Systems know nothing about each other. Each declares only what it offers or needs, in a vocabulary of two words:

a vertical system provides 'lines' or 'points' a horizontal one bears 'lines', 'points' or 'either'

Five of the nine vertical systems provide points, four provide lines. Of the seventeen horizontal systems, nine bear on either, six need points and two need lines. A refused pairing is explained rather than hidden — "Composite steel floor needs point supports — columns. Add a frame, or choose a system that spans onto walls." This is a compatibility rule, not a structural judgement: it says the pairing is not a recognised way to build, not that it would fail.

Connections

The junctions are derived from the pair of materials meeting at them, not chosen. A steel column on concrete gets a bolted base plate and holding-down bolts; a concrete one gets cast-in starter bars over a 75 mm kicker; a timber post gets a galvanised post base. Eight connection types are defined, covering base plates, starter bars, post bases, damp-proof courses, wall plates, padstones, bolted end plates and joist hangers.

These are drawn as geometry and named. Nothing is sized, checked or designed: no bolt is counted, no weld is specified, no bearing stress is verified. The base plate's dimension follows the column it sits under, not a calculation.

Build-ups

Nineteen build-ups drive both the 3D wall and the layer diagram from one array, so the drawing and the model cannot disagree. A wall really is built as its stack of layers, each its own object in its own material, which is what lets the explode pull it apart.

Cladding on a framemmMaterial
Rainscreen panel20metal
Ventilated cavity50void
Rigid insulation120insul
Sheathing board12board

 367 mm overall, outside in. Layers carry a key that the system's parameters write to, so changing "Insulation" on the panel edits this stack rather than a separate number.

A layer may be marked within another, meaning it occupies the same space rather than adding to the thickness. The timber platform wall totals 397.5 mm if you add every layer, but 257.5 mm as built, because its 140 mm of insulation sits inside the 140 mm studs. Thickening that insulation past the stud depth changes the drawing in a way that ought to make a student ask what really happens on site.

Sections

111 catalogue sections across steel, concrete, timber and masonry. Steel is tabulated from EN 10365 and searched for the first section deep enough; concrete, glulam, CLT and masonry sizes are generated to the nearest sensible increment (25 mm for concrete, 45 mm — one lamination — for glulam). Fifteen materials carry E, ν, density, fy and γM.

Those material properties are stored but barely used. Density does not feed the load takedown, which works from the use-class allowance instead; E appears nowhere, because nothing computes a deflection. They are there for display and for whatever the tool might do later.

Edits

An element you change is remembered by where it sits on the grid, not by an index, so the edit survives you altering the building around it. When a change destroys the position an edit belonged to, the app says so and offers to keep or discard it rather than silently forgetting.

What is and isn't modelled

Included

In
Preliminary member sizing
27 rules covering steel, concrete, timber and masonry, each with a stated source, a span range it suits, and the arithmetic shown on screen as it is worked.
In
Gravity load takedown
Tributary area per support, accumulated storey by storey, to line loads under walls or point loads under columns. Both directions are asserted to balance.

At the foot of any inclined member, H = V/tan(pitch), computed from the member's real geometry. This is why the tool can show you that a pitched roof spreads.
In

h_ef/t_ef against 27, or 20 for thin leaves in two storeys or more, with the cavity-wall effective thickness ⅔(t₁+t₂). The only code check in the application.
In

Pad area from bearing pressure, strip width from load per metre, raft thickness from span, pile count from an assumed 600 kN per pile. Soil bearing capacity is an input.

19 layer stacks, each layer its own object at its real thickness, driving the model and the drawing from one source.
Partly
The green / amber / red flag
It compares the span against the range the rule suits — and, for the masonry and pad rules, a ratio against its limit. It is not a capacity check, and a green flag does not mean the member works.
Partly
Load-path shares
A member's strip of floor comes from its own family spacing where the generator recorded one, and otherwise from half the smaller grid dimension — a guess, flagged internally as inferred but not surfaced in the interface.

Not modelled

These are the assumptions a student is most likely to make wrongly, because a general-purpose structural tool would handle them.

Out
Structural analysis
There is no solver of any kind: no stiffness matrix, no finite elements, no moment, no shear force, no bending diagram. Nothing in the application computes an internal force.
Out
Strength and deflection checks
No member is ever verified against a capacity or a deflection limit. Several rules note in their source text that deflection normally governs — and then do not check it.
Out
Buckling, fire and connection design
Named in the tool's own disclaimer as absent, and absent they are. Column sizing uses a flat permissible stress with no slenderness reduction whatsoever.
Out
Lateral load
No wind, no seismic, no notional horizontal force. Braced frames, shear walls and portal frames are drawn and named, but nothing sizes their bracing or checks the building's stability.
Out
Snow and load combinations
One combined allowance per square metre per use class, at service level. Dead and imposed are never separated, no partial factors are applied, and no combination is formed.
Out
Reinforcement
No bar is sized, counted or arranged. Concrete elements are solid volumes; the starter bars at a column base are drawn as geometry at a fixed arrangement.
Out
Building physics
No U-value, no thermal bridge, no condensation risk, no acoustics, no airtightness. The insulation layer in a build-up has a thickness and a colour and nothing else. For that, use the appropriate tool — this one has no material conductivity data at all.
Out
Cost and carbon
No quantities are priced and no embodied carbon is calculated, though the element counts a take-off would need are present in the model.
Out
Drawing your own elements
The document reserves a manual array described in the source as "elements the student drew", and nothing in the application ever writes to it. There is no drawing tool. Everything you see is generated or edited, never drawn.
Note
Geometry
One rectangular footprint on an orthogonal grid. No L-plans, no curves, no setbacks, no atria, no double-height spaces, no stairs or cores as modelled objects.
04

How exact the numbers are

"Accuracy" is the wrong frame for a tool with no analysis in it: there is no exact answer to be near. What can be stated is how the arithmetic behaves, and where it quietly stops telling you things. Every figure below was measured by driving the tool's own sizing functions, not estimated.

The flag is green far more often than it should be

Eight of the 27 rules can never return anything but green, because they carry neither a span band to compare against nor a real check:

found.padIt does have a check, but the check is written to return a constant zero against a limit of one. It can only ever pass.
 Only masonry.slender performs a genuine check. The other 18 rules compare the span against the range the rule suits — useful, but a statement about applicability, not about the member.

Conservative in one direction, unconservative in another

high
No imposed-load reduction
Every storey is counted at its full allowance. Real practice reduces the imposed component on a column gathering many floors, so the tool's loads run high — increasingly so with height.
low
No slenderness reduction
Column area uses a flat permissible stress with no reduction for buckling. A slender column is therefore undersized by this rule, in the opposite direction to the point above. The two do not cancel in any predictable way.
±
One combined load
Dead and imposed are never separated and no partial factors are applied, so the figures are neither characteristic nor design values. They cannot be carried into a code calculation as they stand.
±
Rounding
Concrete to 25 mm, glulam to 45 mm — one lamination — masonry to 5 mm, and catalogue families to the next section up. Panels are synthesised at the exact size wanted, so a CLT or hollowcore depth can look more precise than the rule behind it.

What is actually asserted

The parts of the arithmetic that can be checked, are, in an automated suite: 27 sizing checks against hand calculation, 18 load-takedown checks in both directions including the eaves thrust, the generator exercised across all 30 systems and every cross-part combination, and 71 checks driving the app in a real browser. That tells you the tool computes what it says it computes. It says nothing about whether a rule of thumb suits your building.

Per-system parameters

Thirty-one distinct parameters across the thirty systems — stud centres, insulation thickness, slab depth over deck, secondary spacing, purlin centres, roof pitch, eaves overhang, structural topping, rib module, and so on. Each is declared with its own range and default by the system that owns it, and appears only when that system is selected.

Several write straight into a build-up layer rather than into a separate number — "Insulation" edits the insulation layer of the wall stack itself. This is why the drawing and the model can never disagree, and why changing insulation past the stud depth visibly changes the wall rather than being silently absorbed.

Controls that change nothing numerical

Explode, Open, the section box, the four display styles, layer isolation, labels, the build sequence and the comparison view are all presentation. None of them touches a calculation. The only thing that changes the numbers is the building itself: systems, grid, storeys, height, use class and soil.

06

Compared with Revit, Tekla and a hand calculation

Where it should agree

  • A hand takedown. The tributary-area calculation is the one taught in first-year structures, and the tool performs exactly it. A student's arithmetic and the tool's should match to rounding.
  • Span-to-depth rules of thumb. The divisors come from Allen & Iano's tables and the standard steel and concrete references, so a depth checked against those books will agree — provided you use the midpoint the tool uses rather than either end of the printed range.
  • Geometry and quantities. The model is dimensionally real. Element counts, member lengths and layer thicknesses are as drawn, and would survive a take-off.
  • Eaves thrust. H = V/tan(pitch) is the textbook result, and the tool computes it from the actual member geometry rather than a lookup.

Where it will diverge

  • Any analysis at all. Revit with a structural analysis link, Tekla, Robot or SCIA compute internal forces; this computes none. There is no setting that closes that gap because there is no solver to configure.
  • Continuity and frame action. A real frame sheds load through continuity and moment connections. Every support here takes only what falls nearest to it, so interior columns are under-loaded and edge columns over-loaded relative to an analysed frame.
  • Lateral stability. Tekla and Revit model bracing and cores as load-carrying; here they are drawn but carry nothing. Nothing sizes a brace.
  • Detailing. Tekla exists to produce fabrication-level connections with every bolt and weld. This tool names a connection type and draws its shape.
  • Tall or heavily loaded buildings. As §04 shows, the steel catalogue is exhausted from around eight storeys on a 7.5 m grid, and the tool does not say so.
  • Building physics. Not a comparison at all — there is no thermal, moisture or acoustic model of any kind, so nothing here can be set against WUFI, THERM or an ISO 6946 calculation.

The honest summary: this tool sits between a sketch and a scheme. It is more rigorous than a sketch because the geometry is real and the arithmetic balances, and far less rigorous than a scheme design because nothing is checked. Use it to understand relationships — how depth follows span, how load accumulates, how a system choice propagates through a building — and use something else to find out whether the building stands up.

07

Three exercises

Each isolates one variable and holds everything else fixed. Start from a template, change only what the step names, and read the sizing panel rather than the picture.

Exercise 01

Depth follows span, and it is not a straight line you can see

  1. Load the office template. Set the grid to 1 × 1 bays so there is one span to watch, floor system Composite steel floor.
  2. Set the span to 6.0 m. Click a secondary beam and write down the depth the panel computes and the section it chooses.
  3. Repeat at 6.5, 7.0, 7.75, 8.5 and 8.75 m. Six readings.
  4. Plot computed depth against span, and the chosen section against span, on the same axes.
  5. Now switch the floor to Timber joist and repeat at three spans. Note where the amber flag appears and read what it says.

Principle. The rule is a straight line — depth is span over a constant. The answer is a staircase, because real sections come in sizes. Two buildings 0.5 m apart in span can take the identical beam, and two 0.25 m apart can differ by a whole size. This is why a structural grid is chosen, not derived, and why the question "what span should I use?" has flat spots in it worth landing on. The timber run adds the second lesson: every rule has a range it suits, and the flag is telling you about the span, not about the beam.

Exercise 02

The same floor, carried four ways

  1. Office template, 7.5 × 7.5 m grid, 3 storeys, use class Office.
  2. Open Teach → Compare and put Composite steel floor against RC flat slab. Record the structural depth of each and the floor-to-floor consequence.
  3. Swap the comparison to Precast hollowcore and then CLT floor panels. Record both.
  4. For each of the four, open Analysis and read the load arriving at one internal column. Note that three of the four give you the same number.
  5. Now change the use class to Industrial and re-read one column. Then change it back.

Principle. Floor system choice is a depth decision long before it is a strength decision — over three storeys, 200 mm of structural depth is most of a storey height. The column load barely moves between systems because the tool loads the floor by use class, not by what the floor is made of: the self-weight of a concrete slab and a CLT panel are identical to this model. That is a limitation, and finding it yourself is the exercise. Ask what the answer should look like, then look at §01 to see why it does not.

Exercise 03

Where the roof pushes, and what stops it

  1. Load the shed template — a steel portal frame. Open Analysis and turn on the load-path arrows.
  2. Filter to inclined members only. Read the thrust at the foot of a rafter, and the pitch it reports.
  3. Change the roof pitch from 6° to 15°, the maximum. Re-read the thrust.
  4. Now set it to the minimum, 3°, and read it again. Write down all three, with their pitches.
  5. Switch the roof to Trussed rafter roof on a house template and look at what carries that push instead.

Principle. H = V/tan(pitch) is a hyperbola, not a line: halving the pitch roughly doubles the horizontal push, and as pitch approaches zero the thrust runs away. A shallow pitch looks cheap in roof area and is expensive in everything that has to resist the spread — which is why portal frames have haunches, why trusses have a tie along the bottom, and why a cut roof needs either a tie or a wall stiff enough to be pushed. The arrows make a force you can only otherwise infer into something you can see.

A caution for all three: quote differences, not absolute values. These are rules of thumb rounded to catalogue sizes. A change that moves a section by one size is a result; a 5% change in a computed depth is the rule's own imprecision, not a finding.

Everything on this page was read from the tool's source code, and the measured tables in §04 were produced by driving its own sizing functions. Where a figure is quoted it is measured, not estimated. Where the code did not make something clear it has been said so rather than inferred.

For anything that must stand up, use an engineer and an analysis package. This tool is built to teach relationships and orders of magnitude, and it contains no structural analysis at all — deliberately, so that what it does do stays legible.
